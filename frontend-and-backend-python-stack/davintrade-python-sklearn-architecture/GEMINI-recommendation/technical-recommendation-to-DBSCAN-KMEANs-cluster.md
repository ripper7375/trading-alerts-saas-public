This is an exceptionally well-designed architectural blueprint. Transitioning the mathematical heavy lifting out of MQL5 and into a dedicated Python environment is exactly the right move for a platform of this scale.
Your reasoning for moving away from pure K-Means is spot on. The two-stage DBSCAN-seeded K-Means pipeline solves the initialization trap (Lloyd's algorithm local minima) beautifully. More importantly, your insight regarding the Noise Secondary Classification—recognizing that statistical outliers in financial data represent high-value liquidity pools and untested S/R levels rather than just "bad data"—is brilliant. It takes standard machine learning and correctly adapts it to market microstructure.
Here are my technical recommendations and a few edge cases to consider as you feed this blueprint to your AI coding assistants to build out the DavinTrade backend:

1. Data Ingestion & Database Bottlenecks
   • The Bottleneck: Your pipeline fetches up to 1,000 recent fractal points from PostgreSQL every time the Celery worker fires. If you scale to 5–15 MT5 terminals across dozens of symbols and multiple timeframes, querying the SQL database that frequently (especially on lower timeframes like M15) will cause high I/O load.
   • Recommendation: Use PostgreSQL as your permanent source of truth, but consider maintaining a rolling window of the last 1,000 fractals directly in a Redis Sorted Set or List for each symbol:timeframe. Your Celery worker can pull the array from memory via Redis instantly, bypassing the PostgreSQL disk read entirely for the cluster computation.
2. DBSCAN Epsilon ($\varepsilon$) Auto-Estimation Volatility
   • The Edge Case: Your Python plan uses the $k$-NN distance elbow method (using np.diff) to automatically find the optimal epsilon. Financial data is notoriously noisy, meaning the $k$-distance graph might not have a single, smooth elbow; it might have multiple micro-elbows, causing np.argmax(np.abs(np.diff(diffs))) to pick an erratic epsilon from bar to bar.
   • Recommendation: Apply a smoothing filter (like a 1D Gaussian filter or a simple moving average) to your k_distances array before calculating the derivatives. This will stabilize the $\varepsilon$ estimation and prevent your clusters from violently merging or splitting as the 1,000-bar window shifts.
3. Normalization & Spatial Shifting
   • The Edge Case: In Stage 1, you normalize the bar_index ($X$-axis) and price ($Y$-axis) to a $[0, 1]$ scale. Because the 1,000-bar window rolls forward continuously, the $X$-coordinate of an older fractal will slowly shift closer to $0$. This means the spatial distance between points changes slightly with every new bar, which could occasionally cause a fractal on the edge of a cluster to "fall out" and be reclassified.
   • Recommendation: In your K-Means/DBSCAN distance metric, consider applying a fractional weight to the $X$-axis (time) compared to the $Y$-axis (price). For Support/Resistance, horizontal price alignment is vastly more important than temporal proximity. Scaling the normalized $X$-axis by $0.5$ or $0.3$ will keep your clusters tightly bound to price levels even as time advances.
4. Celery Concurrency & Race Conditions
   • The Edge Case: If market volatility spikes, multiple timeframes for the same symbol might trigger Celery tasks almost simultaneously.
   • Recommendation: Ensure your Celery workers are configured with worker_prefetch_multiplier = 1 so that long-running calculations on a higher timeframe (like H4) don't block a queue of faster M15 updates.
